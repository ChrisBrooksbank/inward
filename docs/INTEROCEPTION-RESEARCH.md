## Executive Summary

Interoception—the sense of the internal state of the body—is increasingly recognized as foundational to emotional awareness, self-regulation, and mental health. Research over the past two decades has established that interoceptive abilities vary significantly across individuals and clinical populations, and crucially, that these abilities are trainable.

This report synthesizes findings from randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews, and clinical protocols to identify evidence-based approaches for improving interoceptive awareness. Key findings include:

- **Interoception has three distinct dimensions** (accuracy, sensibility, awareness) that can be independently targeted
- **Training is effective**: Meta-analyses show moderate effect sizes (g=0.31) for body-focused interventions
- **Clinical relevance is high**: Improved interoception reduces anxiety symptoms by 31% in autistic adults
- **Short practices work**: Effective protocols use sessions under 60 seconds, repeated daily
- **Vocabulary matters**: Building language for internal sensations is a validated training mechanism

These findings directly inform the design of digital interventions for interoceptive training.

## What is Interoception?

### Definition

Interoception refers to the process by which the nervous system senses, interprets, and integrates signals originating from within the body. Often called the "eighth sense" (after the traditional five plus vestibular and proprioceptive senses), interoception provides a moment-to-moment mapping of the body's internal landscape.

The term was coined by Charles Sherrington in 1906 to distinguish internal body signals from exteroception (external stimuli) and proprioception (body position in space).

### Key Interoceptive Signals

| Signal Type     | Body Systems           | Example Sensations                       |
| --------------- | ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| **Cardiac**     | Heart, blood vessels   | Heartbeat, pulse, blood pressure changes |
| **Respiratory** | Lungs, diaphragm       | Breath depth, air hunger, breathlessness |
| **Gastric**     | Stomach, intestines    | Hunger, fullness, nausea, "gut feelings" |
| **Thermal**     | Skin, core temperature | Hot, cold, sweating, flushing            |
| **Nociceptive** | Pain receptors         | Aches, sharp pain, dull pain, cramping   |
| **Muscular**    | Skeletal muscles       | Tension, fatigue, trembling              |
| **Affective**   | Whole body             | Emotional feelings, arousal states       |

### The Body-Emotion Connection

A critical insight from interoception research is the bidirectional relationship between body signals and emotions. The James-Lange theory of emotion (1884) proposed that emotions are the perception of bodily changes—we don't tremble because we're afraid; we feel afraid because we perceive ourselves trembling.

Modern research supports a more nuanced view: interoceptive signals contribute to emotional experience through predictive processing. The brain constantly predicts expected body states and generates emotional responses when predictions don't match actual signals.

This connection explains why:

- People with poor interoceptive awareness often struggle to identify emotions (alexithymia)
- Anxiety involves hypervigilance to threatening body signals
- Mindfulness practices that enhance body awareness also improve emotional regulation

## The Three Dimensions Framework

Sarah Garfinkel and colleagues (2015) proposed that interoception comprises three distinct but related dimensions. This framework has become foundational to research and clinical practice.

### 1. Interoceptive Accuracy (IAcc)

**Definition**: Objective performance on behavioral tasks that measure detection of internal signals.

**Measurement**: Heartbeat detection tasks, respiratory load detection, gastric signal detection.

**Key characteristics**:

- Can be objectively measured
- Shows significant individual variation (range: 0.2-0.95 on heartbeat tasks)
- Correlates with insula cortex activity
- Can be trained with feedback-based protocols

**Example**: In a heartbeat detection task, a participant with high interoceptive accuracy correctly identifies whether tones are synchronous or asynchronous with their heartbeat on >75% of trials.

### 2. Interoceptive Sensibility (IS)

**Definition**: Self-reported subjective experience of interoceptive attention and awareness.

**Measurement**: Questionnaires like MAIA-2, Body Perception Questionnaire, Porges Body Perception Questionnaire.

**Key characteristics**:

- Reflects beliefs about one's body awareness
- May not correlate with actual accuracy
- Influenced by attention, mood, and context
- Multiple subscales capture different aspects (noticing, trusting, regulating)

**Example**: A person with high interoceptive sensibility might strongly agree with statements like "I notice where in my body I am comfortable" or "I can notice an unpleasant body sensation without worrying about it."

### 3. Interoceptive Awareness (IAw)

**Definition**: Metacognitive insight into one's interoceptive accuracy—knowing how good you are at detecting internal signals.

**Measurement**: Correspondence between confidence ratings and actual performance on detection tasks.

**Key characteristics**:

- Represents metacognitive accuracy
- Calculated as correlation between trial-by-trial confidence and performance
- Low awareness = overconfidence or underconfidence
- Critical for adaptive behavior (knowing when to trust gut feelings)

**Example**: A person with high interoceptive awareness has accurate confidence—they're confident when they're right and uncertain when they're guessing.

### The Mismatch Hypothesis

Garfinkel's most clinically significant finding is that anxiety is associated with discrepancies between dimensions, particularly:

- **High sensibility + Low accuracy** = heightened attention to unreliable signals
- **Low awareness** = inability to calibrate confidence appropriately

This "mismatch" pattern has been observed in:

- Generalized anxiety disorder
- Panic disorder
- Autistic individuals with co-occurring anxiety

The implication: effective treatment may need to either improve accuracy to match sensibility, or recalibrate sensibility to match accuracy.

## Measurement Methods

### Heartbeat Detection Task (HDT)

**Protocol**: Participants judge whether external tones are synchronous or asynchronous with their heartbeat. Tones are presented at varying delays from the R-wave of the ECG (0-500ms).

**Advantages**:

- Well-validated across populations
- Provides objective accuracy score
- Confidence ratings enable awareness calculation

**Limitations**:

- Requires ECG equipment
- Performance influenced by heart rate variability
- May measure timing perception rather than pure interoception

### Heartbeat Counting/Mental Tracking Task

**Protocol**: Participants silently count their heartbeats over fixed intervals (e.g., 25, 35, 45 seconds) without taking pulse. Accuracy = 1 - (|reported - actual| / actual).

**Advantages**:

- Simple to administer
- No special equipment beyond heart rate monitor
- Face valid ("count your heartbeats")

**Limitations**:

- Significant validity concerns
- Participants may use time estimation strategies
- Knowledge of resting heart rate biases responses
- Poor test-retest reliability

### Multi-Interval Methods

**6-Alternative Forced Choice (6AFC)**: Participants choose which of six intervals contains heartbeat-synchronous tones. Uses adaptive staircasing to find detection thresholds.

**Method of Constant Stimuli (MCS)**: Multiple trials at fixed delays, plotted as psychometric function to derive detection thresholds.

**Advantages**:

- More sensitive than binary tasks
- Provide threshold estimates
- Better psychometric properties

### MAIA-2 Scale

The Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness Version 2 (Mehling et al., 2018) is the gold-standard self-report measure.

**8 Subscales**:

| Subscale                 | Description                                       | Example Item                                                         |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Noticing**             | Awareness of body sensations                      | "I notice when I am uncomfortable"                                   |
| **Not-Distracting**      | Not ignoring/distracting from discomfort          | "I try to ignore pain" (reverse scored)                              |
| **Not-Worrying**         | Not worrying about uncomfortable sensations       | "I can feel unpleasant in my body without worrying"                  |
| **Attention Regulation** | Ability to sustain and control attention to body  | "I can maintain awareness of my whole body"                          |
| **Emotional Awareness**  | Connection between body and emotional states      | "I notice body changes when I'm upset"                               |
| **Self-Regulation**      | Ability to regulate distress by attention to body | "When something feels bad, I can think about breathing to calm down" |
| **Body Listening**       | Active listening to body for insight              | "I listen to my body to inform me about what to do"                  |
| **Trusting**             | Trust in body signals as safe and trustworthy     | "I feel my body is a safe place"                                     |

**Scoring**: 37 items rated 0-5, averaged within subscales. No total score—subscales examined separately.

**Psychometrics**: Strong internal consistency (α = 0.66-0.87), validated across languages and populations.

## Clinical Populations

### Anxiety Disorders

**Pattern**: High interoceptive sensibility with variable accuracy; low interoceptive awareness.

**Mechanism**: Hypervigilance to body signals without accurate detection leads to misinterpretation of benign sensations as threatening. A racing heart from exercise is interpreted as panic; stomach sensations become proof of serious illness.

**Research findings**:

- Panic disorder shows heightened cardiac sensitivity during panic but not at baseline
- GAD associated with "interoceptive noise"—difficulty distinguishing signal from noise
- Treatment with ADIE therapy reduces anxiety symptoms through improved accuracy

### Autism Spectrum Conditions

**Pattern**: Highly variable—some autistic individuals show enhanced accuracy, others show reduced. Often high sensibility coupled with difficulties interpreting signals.

**Mechanism**: Atypical interoceptive processing contributes to alexithymia (40% co-occurrence in autism vs 10% general population), sensory overwhelm, and emotional regulation difficulties.

**Research findings**:

- 65% of autistic adults with anxiety showed interoceptive atypicalities
- Garfinkel's ADIE RCT: 6-session training improved accuracy from 0.48 to 0.76
- Training reduced anxiety severity with 31% meeting recovery criteria

### ADHD

**Pattern**: Reduced interoceptive accuracy, particularly for subtle signals; difficulty sustaining attention to body.

**Mechanism**: Executive function deficits impair ability to maintain focus on internal signals. Hypoawareness may contribute to poor self-regulation (not noticing hunger, fatigue, need to urinate).

**Research findings**:

- Reduced heartbeat detection accuracy compared to controls
- MAIA subscales Attention Regulation and Self-Regulation particularly affected
- Mindfulness training shows promise in improving interoceptive attention

### Alexithymia

**Pattern**: Fundamental difficulty identifying and describing feelings; poor interoceptive accuracy across modalities.

**Mechanism**: Alexithymia may be a consequence of impaired interoception—if you can't detect body signals that constitute emotions, you can't identify what you're feeling.

**Research findings**:

- Strong negative correlation between alexithymia and interoceptive accuracy
- Vocabulary building interventions improve both interoception and alexithymia
- 10% population prevalence; 40% in autism, eating disorders, trauma

### Eating Disorders

**Pattern**: Disrupted gastric interoception; confusion between hunger, fullness, and emotional states.

**Mechanism**: Difficulty distinguishing hunger from anxiety or emptiness as emotional state leads to dysregulated eating. Fullness signals may be perceived as threatening.

**Research findings**:

- Anorexia shows reduced accuracy for gastric signals
- Bulimia shows atypical gut-brain signaling
- Interoceptive training being integrated into treatment protocols

### PTSD and Trauma

**Pattern**: Hyper- or hypo-sensitivity to body signals; body perceived as unsafe.

**Mechanism**: Trauma often involves overwhelming body experiences. Defense responses may include disconnection from body (dissociation) or constant vigilance for threat signals.

**Research findings**:

- MAIA subscale "Trusting" particularly impaired
- Trauma-sensitive approaches emphasize gradual, safe reconnection
- Somatic therapies (SE, EMDR) work through interoceptive processing

## Evidence-Based Training Interventions

### 6.1 Body Scan Meditation

**Protocol**:

- Duration: 15-20 minutes per session
- Frequency: Daily practice
- Total program: 8 weeks typically
- Method: Systematic attention through body regions, noticing sensations without judgment

**Typical sequence**:

1. Feet and toes (2-3 min)
2. Lower legs, knees (2-3 min)
3. Upper legs, hips (2-3 min)
4. Pelvis, lower abdomen (2-3 min)
5. Upper abdomen, chest (2-3 min)
6. Hands, arms (2-3 min)
7. Shoulders, neck (2-3 min)
8. Face, head (2-3 min)

**Evidence**:

- Meta-analysis of mindfulness interventions: effect size g=0.31 for interoceptive outcomes
- fMRI studies show increased insula activation after training
- Improvements maintained at 3-month follow-up

**Mechanism**:

- Experience-dependent plasticity in interoceptive cortex
- Improved attention regulation through practice
- Reduced avoidance of body sensations
- "Noticing without suppression" builds tolerance

### 6.2 ADIE Therapy (Garfinkel Protocol)

**Protocol**:

- Sessions: 6 individual sessions
- Duration: 60-90 minutes each
- Frequency: Weekly
- Focus: Heartbeat detection with feedback training

**Session structure**:

1. Baseline heartbeat detection assessment
2. Psychoeducation about interoception and anxiety
3. Attention training to cardiac signals
4. Detection practice with real-time feedback
5. Confidence calibration exercises
6. Generalization to daily life

**Evidence**:

- RCT with 121 autistic adults with anxiety
- Accuracy improved from 0.48 to 0.76 (large effect)
- 31% met anxiety recovery criteria vs 16% control
- Effects maintained at 6-month follow-up

**Key innovations**:

- Real-time feedback on detection accuracy
- Explicit metacognitive training (calibrating confidence)
- Integration of interoception with anxiety management

### 6.3 Kelly Mahler's Interoception Curriculum

**Overview**: An 8-phase curriculum designed for classroom and clinical use, particularly with autistic individuals and those with developmental differences.

**Structure**:

- **Phase 1-3**: Body lessons (single body part focus)
- **Phase 4-5**: Emotion lessons (connecting sensations to feelings)
- **Phase 6-8**: Action lessons (using interoception for self-regulation)

**Body lesson example (Phase 2)**:

1. Name the body part (e.g., "stomach")
2. Locate it on a body map
3. Notice it during different activities (rest, movement, eating)
4. Describe sensations in own words
5. Compare to peers' descriptions

**Key principles**:

- Activities under 60 seconds
- No special equipment required
- Focus on one body part at a time
- Build personal vocabulary (not prescribed words)
- Regular, brief practice over long sessions

**Evidence**:

- Validated with autistic children and adolescents
- Improvements in emotional awareness and self-regulation
- Teachers report increased student self-advocacy
- Published curriculum with research support

### 6.4 Mindfulness-Based Interventions

#### MBSR (Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction)

**Protocol**:

- 8 weekly 2.5-hour sessions
- 1 full-day retreat
- Daily home practice (45 min)
- Includes body scan, sitting meditation, mindful movement

**Evidence for interoception**:

- Increased MAIA scores across subscales
- Enhanced insula grey matter density
- Improved heartbeat detection in some studies

#### Yoga

**Mechanism**: Combines movement (amplifying signals), breath focus (respiratory interoception), and sustained attention.

**Evidence**:

- Systematic review shows moderate effects on interoceptive awareness
- Particular benefits for Attention Regulation and Body Listening subscales
- Active practice may be more effective than passive meditation alone

#### Tai Chi

**Evidence**:

- Improvements in proprioceptive and interoceptive awareness
- Benefits for older adults' body awareness
- Slow movements allow sustained attention to subtle signals

### 6.5 Biofeedback Training

#### Heart Rate Variability (HRV) Biofeedback

**Protocol**:

- 5-10 sessions
- Real-time display of heart rate and HRV
- Training to increase HRV through breath and relaxation
- Gradual withdrawal of visual feedback

**Evidence**:

- Improvements in cardiac interoceptive accuracy
- Reduced anxiety symptoms
- Enhanced self-regulation capacity

#### Gastric Biofeedback

**Protocol**:

- Electrogastrography (EGG) feedback
- Training to detect gastric rhythms
- Emerging application for eating disorders

**Evidence**:

- 5-day protocols show improved gastric awareness
- Promising for populations with disrupted eating

### 6.6 Physical Activity Approaches

**Rationale**: Exercise temporarily amplifies interoceptive signals (heart rate, breathing, temperature), making detection easier. This "signal amplification" provides training opportunities for those who struggle to detect subtle signals at rest.

**Protocols**:

- Brief exercise (jumping jacks, stairs) before attention practice
- Attention to body during cool-down period
- Comparison of "active" vs "resting" sensations

**Evidence**:

- Enhanced detection accuracy immediately post-exercise
- May accelerate learning for low-accuracy individuals
- Recommended as component of multi-modal interventions

## Key Training Principles

Synthesizing across interventions, seven principles emerge for effective interoceptive training:

### 1. Focus on Single Body Regions

**Principle**: Attention to one body part at a time prevents overwhelm and builds skills incrementally.

**Evidence**: Mahler's curriculum success with single-part focus; body scan systematic progression; ADIE's cardiac-specific training.

**Application**: Exercises should target specific regions (stomach, chest, hands) rather than "whole body awareness."

### 2. Use Physical Activity to Amplify Signals

**Principle**: Movement increases signal intensity, making detection easier for beginners.

**Evidence**: Post-exercise accuracy improvements; yoga and tai chi benefits; exercise as "training wheels" for interoception.

**Application**: Include movement-based activities, especially early in training; use active-rest comparisons.

### 3. Practice Non-Judgmental Noticing

**Principle**: Observe sensations without trying to change, suppress, or interpret them.

**Evidence**: Mindfulness mechanisms; "Not-Worrying" as protective factor; acceptance-based approaches outperform control-based.

**Application**: Explicit instruction to "just notice" without labeling sensations as good/bad or trying to relax.

### 4. Build Personal Vocabulary

**Principle**: Developing words for sensations enables recognition and communication.

**Evidence**: Alexithymia improvement through vocabulary; MAIA Emotional Awareness subscale; peer vocabulary sharing.

**Application**: Encourage personal descriptions over prescribed terms; exposure to others' sensation words.

### 5. Connect Sensations to Emotional States

**Principle**: Explicit linking of body signals to emotions builds emotional awareness.

**Evidence**: MAIA Emotional Awareness as outcome; Mahler's emotion lessons; body-emotion connection fundamental.

**Application**: After noticing body sensations, prompt reflection on emotional correlates.

### 6. Practice Short and Frequently

**Principle**: Brief, regular practice outperforms long, infrequent sessions.

**Evidence**: Mahler's <60-second activities; daily practice more effective than weekly; habit formation research.

**Application**: Design for micro-practices (30-60 seconds); emphasize daily consistency over session length.

### 7. Progress from Strong to Subtle Signals

**Principle**: Start with easily detectable sensations; gradually refine to subtle signals.

**Evidence**: Exercise amplification for beginners; progressive difficulty in detection tasks; building from gross to fine awareness.

**Application**: Sequence training from high-intensity (post-exercise heart rate) to low-intensity (resting subtle sensations).

## Implications for Inward App

The research literature provides clear guidance for designing an evidence-based interoceptive training app:

### Exercise Design

1. **Single body part focus**: Each exercise should target one region (stomach, chest, hands, feet, shoulders, etc.)
2. **Movement integration**: Include activities that involve brief physical exertion before noticing
3. **Micro-duration**: Core exercises under 60 seconds; longer sessions optional
4. **Progressive difficulty**: Start with post-activity amplified signals; advance to resting detection

### Progress Measurement

1. **MAIA-2 subscales as outcomes**: Track Noticing, Attention Regulation, Emotional Awareness, Body Listening, Trusting
2. **Avoid false precision**: Self-report trends more meaningful than pseudo-objective scores
3. **Dimensional approach**: Measure multiple aspects independently

### Vocabulary Building

1. **Personal terminology**: Encourage users' own words rather than prescribed vocabulary
2. **P2P sharing scientifically supported**: "Discovering new words for experiences" aligns with alexithymia intervention research
3. **Confirmation mechanism**: "Yes, I feel this too" validates shared vocabulary

### Target Populations

Literature clearly defines relevant populations:

- Anxiety disorders (particularly with interoceptive mismatch)
- Autistic individuals (40% alexithymia co-occurrence)
- ADHD (attention regulation deficits)
- Trauma survivors (body reconnection needed)
- Alexithymia (primary vocabulary deficit)

### Mechanism of Change

The app should target:

1. **Attention skills**: Sustained, controlled focus on body regions
2. **Vocabulary development**: Words for previously ineffable experiences
3. **Body-emotion linking**: Explicit connection of sensations to feelings
4. **Reduced avoidance**: Comfortable, non-judgmental engagement with body
